# The survival kit

The highest-leverage thing a near-beginner can memorise. These phrases don't let you *say* more — they let you **stay in Japanese** when you get stuck, instead of switching to English. That difference is most of what separates "I studied Japanese for a year" from "I can have a conversation."

Learn these before almost any grammar. They're fixed blocks — no conjugation, no parsing. Memorise as sound-units.

## Keeping the conversation alive

| Japanese | English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| もう いちど おねがいします | One more time, please | The single most useful sentence in the language |
| ゆっくり おねがいします | Slowly, please | Combine: もう いちど、ゆっくり おねがいします |
| なんて いいましたか | What did you say? | Slightly more casual than もう いちど |
| ちょっと まってください | Just a moment, please | Buys you thinking time legitimately |
| わかりません | I don't understand | Not "I don't know" — that's しりません |
| わかりました | I understand / got it | Signals *keep going* |
| だいじょうぶです | I'm fine / it's fine / no thanks | Enormously versatile. Also declines politely |

## Getting words you don't have

| Japanese | English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 〜は なんですか | What is 〜? | Point at the thing and say it |
| 〜は にほんごで なんですか | What is 〜 in Japanese? | **Your vocabulary engine.** Use it constantly |
| 〜は どういう いみですか | What does 〜 mean? | For words you heard but don't know |
| これで いいですか | Is this OK / is this right? | Checking you got it right |
| えいごで いいですか | Is English OK? | The escape hatch. Use it last, not first |

## Fillers — do not skip these

| Japanese | English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ええと… | Um… / er… | Buys time **in Japanese** |
| あのう… | Um… (getting attention) | Softer, often opens a request |
| そうですね… | Well… / let me think | Doubles as agreement and as a stall |

Fillers are badly underrated. A silent pause reads as failure; ええと reads as thinking. Same pause, completely different impression — and it stops you reflexively dropping into English.

## Reacting (aizuchi)

Japanese conversation expects the listener to make noise. Silence reads as *not listening*, not as *politely waiting*.

| Japanese | English |
|---|---|
| はい / うん | Yes / mm-hm (うん is casual) |
| そうですか | Is that so? |
| そうなんですか | Oh really? (mild surprise) |
| なるほど | I see / that makes sense |
| へえ | Huh! / wow (genuine interest) |
| ほんとうですか | Really? |

These are free fluency. You can hold up your half of a conversation with aizuchi alone while the other person does the talking — and it sounds engaged rather than lost.

## The ambiguity trap: いい

**いいです is genuinely ambiguous** and causes real misunderstandings:

- コーヒーは いいですか → could be *would you like coffee?*
- いいです → could be *yes please* **or** *no thanks*

Native speakers disambiguate by tone and context. You shouldn't rely on that yet. Be explicit:

- Accepting → **おねがいします**
- Declining → **だいじょうぶです** or **いりません**

## Two-beginner conversation protocol

When both people are near-beginners, agree these rules out loud before you start. They're what make it work rather than collapse:

1. **English is allowed, but only after you've tried the survival kit.** もう いちど first, えいごで いいですか second.
2. **Say your fillers out loud.** No silent stalling.
3. **Aizuchi every few seconds.** Cheap, and it keeps both people engaged.
4. **Ask 〜は にほんごで なんですか whenever you hit a wall.** Every one of these is a word you'll remember, because you wanted it in the moment.
5. **Don't correct each other mid-flow.** Note it, keep going, compare at the end. Mid-sentence correction kills the confidence that makes beginners keep talking.
