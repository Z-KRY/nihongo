# Paired activities — what works, and why

Reference for the `/nihongo-buddy` skill. Read before generating any paired activity.

## The governing rule

**Build from the intersection, never the union.**

If A knows ～ました and B doesn't, an activity using ～ました fails — not "is harder for B", *fails*. B can't parse the input, so B can't respond, so A gets no reply and both conclude they can't do this. One unknown pattern is enough to kill a beginner conversation.

So: intersect the grammar lists in both `me/progress.md` files. Intersect the vocabulary. Build only inside that overlap. The overlap will feel insultingly small. Build there anyway — a conversation that actually completes is worth ten that stall.

## Asymmetry is a feature, not a problem

Where the profiles differ, don't average them — **assign roles.**

- **A knows a pattern B doesn't** → A teaches it. Explaining something you half-know is one of the most effective ways to actually learn it. Give A 3 minutes to teach, then drill B on it together.
- **Levels differ substantially** → stronger partner takes the **asker/leader** role, weaker takes **responder**. Responding is much easier than initiating: the question hands you most of the grammar and vocabulary you need for the answer.
- **Different strengths** (one has better pronunciation, one better vocab) → the pronunciation-stronger reads aloud first as the model; the vocab-stronger runs the quizzing.

## Activity types, easiest first

### 1. Scripted role-play — rung 1

Both read from a written script. Zero production pressure, zero grammar demand.

Sounds too easy to help. It isn't: it builds mouth-memory for rhythm and intonation, and it's the only conversational activity available at near-zero grammar. Especially good when at least one partner has decent pronunciation — the payoff is immediate and audible.

*Escalation:* run it twice, then a third time with one line blanked out for each person to improvise.

### 2. Substitution ping-pong — rung 1

One frame, alternate turns, swap one word each time.

> A: これは ペンです。
> B: これは ほんです。
> A: これは かばんです。

Builds pattern automaticity on almost no vocabulary. Competitive version: first to hesitate or repeat a word loses. Trivially easy to generate, and genuinely effective — the frame becomes automatic while attention stays on the vocabulary.

### 3. Vocabulary quiz on the overlap — rung 1

Quiz each other from the shared deck. Cheap, high-retention, and you catch each other's errors — which is where a buddy beats solo study.

Best on the **intersection**: words both people have already met. Words only one knows become teach-backs (see asymmetry above).

### 4. Read-aloud / shadowing pair — rung 1

Both read the same short passage aloud, then compare: pacing, pitch, where you each stumbled.

Uniquely good when partners have different pronunciation strengths — the stronger one becomes the model, and the feedback is concrete rather than vague. Also a stealth comprehension drill: after reading, each says what they think it meant, then compare.

### 5. Information gap — rung 2

Each partner gets different information and must ask to complete a task. A has a timetable with gaps B can fill, and vice versa.

This is the first activity that forces **genuine question production** — you have to ask because you actually don't know, not because a worksheet said to. Needs question words (なん、どこ、いつ、だれ), so it's the natural first rung-2 activity.

### 6. Crutch-phrase-only conversation — any rung

Have a conversation using **only** the survival kit in `crutch-phrases.md`, plus gestures and pointing. No other Japanese allowed.

Sounds like a party game. It's the most confidence-building thing on this list, because it proves a real exchange is possible *right now* at your current level — which is the belief most beginners are missing. Also drills the exact phrases that keep future conversations from collapsing.

### 7. Teach-back — any rung

One partner teaches the other something they know and the other doesn't. 3 minutes, then the learner has to use it in 3 sentences.

The protégé effect: preparing to teach forces you to find the edges of your own understanding. Best used deliberately on asymmetries the buddy profiles surface.

## Session shape for a pair

A 30-minute buddy session that reliably works:

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 5 min | Warm-up: substitution ping-pong on a known frame |
| 5 min | Vocabulary quiz on the overlap |
| 10 min | Main activity — scripted role-play (rung 1) or information gap (rung 2) |
| 5 min | Teach-back on one asymmetry |
| 5 min | Free-for-all using survival-kit rules; compare notes afterwards |

## Anti-patterns

- **Don't** generate an activity around a grammar point only one partner knows. Intersection, always.
- **Don't** have beginners free-converse with no structure and no script. It stalls in under a minute, and both people conclude they're worse than they are.
- **Don't** let them correct each other mid-sentence. Note and compare at the end.
- **Don't** build activities that need a third fluent speaker to adjudicate. If the pair can't tell whether they got it right, that's what the next Claude session is for — have them log the disagreement.
- **Don't** assume the higher-level partner benefits less. Explaining is how they consolidate; give them the teaching role explicitly.
